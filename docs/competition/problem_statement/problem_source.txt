2026 Asia and Pacific Mathematical Contest in Modeling
Problem B
Dual-modal Temporal Information Fusion Modeling for Converter
Steelmaking Process
Converter steelmaking is a typical production method in the long‑process steel
industry. It is one of the main ways to make high‑quality steel around the world. This
method has high productivity, low energy use and low cost. It greatly helps the steel
industry save energy, reduce emissions and cut production costs.
The oxygen lance plays an important role in converter steelmaking. It blows
high‑pressure and high‑purity oxygen into the liquid steel bath with supersonic gas
jets. Oxygen reacts with impurities in molten iron, such as carbon, silicon, manganese
and phosphorus. These chemical reactions remove carbon, phosphorus and sulfur. In
this way, the equipment adjusts molten‑steel components to get qualified steel
products. It is one of the most useful pieces of equipment for production control in
converter smelting. There is a time delay. Flue gas generated inside the converter must
travel to sampling probes and analytical devices before data can be collected. This
sampling‑transport delay becomes obvious when gas passes through long pipelines.
Flue‑gas analysis works for many kinds of converters and costs little for equipment
maintenance. However, its results are easily affected by calculation mistakes for the
initial carbon content. Delayed flue‑gas data will make the control accuracy poor.
The converter mouth is the only place we can observe during steelmaking. Its
condition directly shows changes of slag inside the converter. It also strongly affects
production safety and the final quality of molten steel. In actual production, a
USB4000 spectrometer collects large amounts of flame spectral data at the converter
mouth. Data are recorded for more than one thousand smelting heats. At the same
time, a Raman flue‑gas analyzer records CO₂ and CO concentration data at the
-1-

flue‑gas bend. Both devices share the same sampling frequency, as shown in Fig. 1.
The spectral sampling time step is 0.5 s. The flame radiation intensity is measured at
2047 wavelength points from 340.535 nm to 1027.678 nm. For each heat, the
full‑process flame spectral data form an intensity matrix of about 1400 × 2047. The
flue‑gas sampling time step is also 0.5 s. For each heat, the full‑process flue‑gas data
form a 1400 × 2 matrix, storing CO₂ and CO concentrations.

Layout of dual-modal information detection equipment for converter steelmaking.
Flame visual signals from the converter mouth reflect real‑time chemical and
physical reactions inside the molten bath. On the other hand, data from the flue‑gas
analyzer are collected after gas flows from converter mouth to sensors. These flue‑gas
data show bath reactions with a time delay. The two kinds of data complement each
other in the time domain: one is real‑time, the other is delayed. To build an intelligent
end‑point control model using both flame and flue‑gas information, we need to make
full use of this complementary feature. We should build a dual‑modal dataset with
time‑alignment functions. Through data fusion modeling, we can predict delayed
flue‑gas information in real time. This can reduce how often sampling probes work in
the flue‑gas system and extend the service life of expensive devices. It provides basic
mathematical support for intelligent steelmaking upgrades.
In this problem, we provide flame‑spectrum and flue‑gas full‑process data from
10 heats from a real steel plant. The format of sample data is shown in Table 1.
Neighboring time points are 0.5 s apart. Complete datasets are given in Attachment 1

The dataset includes full‑process flame‑spectrum data and flue‑gas data for one
converter heat. Concentration values mean instantaneous volume flow rates of CO₂
and CO (unit: m³/s). It also records total oxygen volume blown into the converter
through the oxygen lance (unit: m³).
Based on the problem background and given data, please build mathematical
models to solve the following three problems.
The spectral data in Attachment 1‑10 are discrete values ordered in time and
wavelength. There are outlier points in these data. Please remove outliers and perform
continuous reconstruction for discrete data. Draw continuous 3D spectral figures for
all ten heats.
Cumulative‑oxygen‑consumption data and flue‑gas data from Attachment 1‑10
are discrete time‑series data with outliers. Use mathematical methods to remove
outliers and reconstruct continuous signals. Plot continuous curves for oxygen‑lance
flow rate, cumulative oxygen consumption, CO₂ concentration, CO concentration,
-3-

total CO₂ emission and total CO emission against time. Study how cumulative oxygen
consumption relates to CO₂ concentration, CO concentration, total CO₂ emission and
total CO emission. Requirements: Flue‑gas signals lag behind changes of cumulative
oxygen consumption and lance flow rate. Higher lance flow rates correspond to
smaller time delays for flue‑gas signals. Calculate the time delay of flue‑gas signals
under different oxygen‑lance flow rates.
